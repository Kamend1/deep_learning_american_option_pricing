## Abstract — provisional

American options differ from European options because the holder may exercise before contractual maturity. This flexibility creates an optimal-stopping problem: at each admissible exercise date, the holder compares immediate exercise with the expected discounted continuation value. Except in limited special cases, this removes the closed-form solution and requires numerical valuation.

This project investigates whether deep neural networks can approximate American put values and early-exercise decisions with sufficient accuracy, financial consistency, and computational efficiency to serve as surrogate models for repeated numerical valuation. Synthetic labels will be produced by a high-resolution Cox–Ross–Rubinstein binomial tree and independently validated against QuantLib. A direct multilayer perceptron will be compared with a financially structured model that predicts only the early-exercise premium over the corresponding European Black–Scholes value. A multi-task extension will jointly estimate price and the exercise-versus-continuation decision.

Evaluation will extend beyond average prediction error. It will include segmented errors, financial lower-bound and monotonicity tests, early-exercise-boundary accuracy, out-of-domain deterioration, and inference speed. The objective is not to claim that deep learning discovers a theoretically superior option value, but to test whether it can provide a fast and financially coherent approximation of a clearly specified numerical pricing model.

> The final abstract will be rewritten after the empirical results are available.

# 1. Motivation and continuity with previous work

This project continues my earlier SoftUni *Math for Developers* final project, [Mathematical Foundations of Portfolio Optimization and Hedging](https://github.com/Kamend1/math_for_developers_final_project). That work connected portfolio optimization, Black–Scholes option pricing, protective puts, option Greeks, convexity, and embedded optionality.

The earlier project concentrated on European-style valuation, where Black–Scholes provides a closed-form solution under restrictive assumptions. The present project moves to a harder problem: an American option may be exercised before maturity, so valuation includes a decision problem.

The objective is not to train a large network merely to reproduce a formula that is already exact under its assumptions and computationally cheap. Elbayed and Qadi El Idrissi (2025), for example, show that a dense network can approximate synthetic European put prices generated by Black–Scholes. That is useful as a function-approximation baseline, but it provides limited computational value because the analytical formula already exists.

American options provide a stronger use case. Their early-exercise feature generally requires recursive, PDE-based, or simulation-based numerical methods. A trained neural surrogate could be valuable when the pricing function must be evaluated repeatedly across large portfolios, scenario grids, or risk calculations.

# 2. Research problem

The project studies the mapping

\[
(S,K,T,r,q,\sigma)\longmapsto V_A,
\]

where \(S\) is spot price, \(K\) is strike, \(T\) is time to maturity, \(r\) is the risk-free rate, \(q\) is continuous dividend yield, \(\sigma\) is volatility, and \(V_A\) is the American put value produced by the selected numerical model.

The network will not forecast the underlying asset and will not learn from future market prices. It will approximate a deterministic numerical pricing function over a predefined domain.

> The project evaluates **surrogate numerical pricing**, not market-price forecasting.

A useful model must satisfy three requirements:

1. **Numerical accuracy** — predictions remain close to the benchmark;
2. **Financial consistency** — predictions respect essential economic bounds and shape properties;
3. **Computational efficiency** — inference is faster than repeated numerical valuation.

# 3. Theoretical framework

## 3.1 European put value

Under the Black–Scholes–Merton framework with continuous dividend yield,

\[
P_E=Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2)-Se^{-qT}N(-d_1),
\]

with

\[
d_1=\frac{\ln(S/K)+(r-q+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}},
\qquad
d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt{T},
\]

where \(N(\cdot)\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function (Black and Scholes, 1973; Merton, 1973).

The European value will serve as both a benchmark and a known component of the proposed early-exercise-premium model.

## 3.2 American optimal stopping

At time \(t\), the holder compares intrinsic value

\[
I(S_t)=\max(K-S_t,0)
\]

with continuation value

\[
C(S_t,t)=
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}
\left[
e^{-r\Delta t}V_A(S_{t+\Delta t},t+\Delta t)\mid S_t
\right].
\]

The American put value is

\[
V_A(S_t,t)=\max\left(I(S_t),C(S_t,t)\right).
\]

The early-exercise boundary separates the region where immediate exercise is optimal from the region where continuing to hold the option has greater value.

---

## 3.3 Early-exercise premium

The American value can be decomposed as

\[
V_A=V_E+EEP,
\]

where

\[
EEP=V_A-V_E
\]

is the early-exercise premium. Because the American contract includes all rights available under the European contract,

\[
V_A\geq V_E
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
EEP\geq 0.
\]

This motivates the main model:

\[
\widehat{V}_A
=
V_{BS}
+
\operatorname{Softplus}(g_\theta(x)).
\]

Instead of relearning the complete pricing function, the network estimates only the value created by early exercise. `Softplus` embeds the non-negative-premium condition directly in the output design.

---

## 3.4 Numerical benchmarks

### Cox–Ross–Rubinstein tree

The Cox–Ross–Rubinstein lattice discretizes the underlying process into upward and downward moves. Under risk-neutral valuation, backward induction is applied. At every node, the American value is the maximum of intrinsic and discounted continuation value (Cox, Ross, and Rubinstein, 1979).

The tree is selected as the primary label generator because it is transparent, compatible with early exercise, and suitable for generating both option prices and exercise labels.

### Independent validation

A representative sample of generated prices will be validated against an independent QuantLib pricing engine. The number of tree steps will be selected through a convergence analysis rather than arbitrarily.

### Least-Squares Monte Carlo extension

Longstaff and Schwartz (2001) estimate continuation values by regressing discounted future cash flows on basis functions. A later extension will test whether a neural network can replace the classical polynomial regression.

# 4. Literature review

## 4.1 Direct supervised neural pricing

Ke and Yang (2019) compare MLP and LSTM architectures on a large historical option dataset. Their strongest specification predicts bid and ask values separately, while their LSTM does not outperform the MLP alternatives. The study shows that greater architectural complexity does not automatically create better pricing performance and that target formulation can be as important as architecture.

Elbayed and Qadi El Idrissi (2025) train a dense network on synthetic European put prices generated from Black–Scholes. Their study demonstrates that a conventional DNN can approximate a smooth theoretical pricing function, but the present project treats this as a baseline rather than a sufficient final objective.

## 4.2 Sequential models

Pimentel et al. (2026) use five-day SPX European call-option sequences and a rolling-window design. Their LSTM models outperform Black–Scholes, Heston, and MLP benchmarks at an aggregate level, with gains concentrated mainly in longer maturities. Their SHAP analysis indicates that sequence information can help the model extract short-term volatility dynamics.

Zouaoui and Naas (2023) discuss LSTM and GRU pricing approaches while emphasizing overfitting, data requirements, computational cost, and interpretability.

These findings support recurrent architectures when the inputs are genuinely sequential. The core dataset in this project is a static parameter vector, so an LSTM or GRU will not be used merely to make the architecture appear more advanced.

---

## 4.3 American options, PDE methods, and richer state representations

Pu (2021) compares supervised neural pricing with neural PDE-based methods for European and American options. The thesis reports stronger practical performance from supervised learning in the examined setting and highlights deterioration outside the training domain. This directly motivates mandatory out-of-domain testing in the current project.

Ding, Lu, and Cheung (2025) compress full SPX implied-volatility surfaces with a variational autoencoder and combine the latent representation with option-specific inputs to price QuantLib-generated American puts and Asian options. Their work demonstrates a credible path toward richer market-state inputs.

The present project deliberately begins with constant volatility. Full volatility surfaces, stochastic volatility, jumps, and discrete dividends are future extensions rather than core requirements.

---

## 4.4 Research gap

The literature establishes that neural networks can approximate option-pricing functions, recurrent models can exploit genuine sequential information, and American options require the exercise decision to be represented either explicitly or implicitly.

The intended contribution is narrower than the broad claim that “deep learning can price options”:

> Can a financially structured neural network learn the American early-exercise premium and exercise boundary more accurately and consistently than a generic direct-price network, while delivering a measurable inference-speed advantage over the numerical benchmark?

This combines:

- direct versus residual target design;
- financially constrained outputs;
- exercise-boundary analysis;
- interpolation and extrapolation tests;
- computational benchmarking.

# 5. Research questions

**RQ1.** Can a conventional MLP accurately approximate high-resolution American put prices within a predefined parameter domain?

**RQ2.** Does predicting the early-exercise premium instead of the full American price improve accuracy or training efficiency?

**RQ3.** Do financially motivated output constraints reduce economically impossible predictions?

**RQ4.** Can a multi-task network jointly learn price and the exercise-versus-continuation decision?

**RQ5.** How do errors vary across moneyness, maturity, volatility, interest-rate, and dividend-yield regimes?

**RQ6.** How materially does performance deteriorate outside the training domain?

**RQ7.** Does neural inference provide a meaningful speed advantage over high-resolution numerical valuation?

# 6. Predefined hypotheses

The hypotheses are fixed before model training to reduce retrospective interpretation.

### H1 — Direct pricing approximation
A direct MLP will produce materially lower in-domain pricing errors than using the European Black–Scholes value as a proxy.

### H2 — Premium decomposition
A model trained to predict the early-exercise premium will outperform a model trained to predict the complete American put value.

### H3 — Financial constraints
A constrained premium model will produce fewer violations of non-negativity, intrinsic-value, and European-value lower bounds than an unconstrained direct-price model.

### H4 — Multi-task exercise learning
A network jointly trained on price and exercise classification will estimate the exercise boundary more accurately than a boundary inferred from a price-only model.

### H5 — Computational acceleration
After training, batched neural inference will be substantially faster than repeated high-resolution binomial-tree valuation.

### H6 — Out-of-domain deterioration
All neural models will perform materially worse on parameter regions intentionally excluded from training.

# 8. Planned methodology

## Input and target

The initial feature vector will be

\[
x=\left[\log(S/K),T,r,q,\sigma\right].
\]

The normalized target will be

\[
y=\frac{V_A}{K}.
\]

Log-moneyness and strike normalization reduce an unnecessary absolute price-scale dimension.

## Models

1. European Black–Scholes proxy;
2. direct MLP;
3. early-exercise-premium MLP;
4. constrained premium MLP;
5. multi-task price and exercise model;
6. neural Least-Squares Monte Carlo extension.

## Metrics

- MAE and RMSE;
- normalized MAE;
- median and maximum absolute error;
- \(R^2\);
- exercise precision, recall, and F1-score;
- financial-constraint violation rates;
- inference time.

MAPE will not be the primary measure because it becomes unstable when true option values are close to zero.

---

# 9. Financial-consistency tests

Predicted American put values should satisfy:

\[
\widehat{V}_A\geq 0,
\]

\[
\widehat{V}_A\geq \max(K-S,0),
\]

\[
\widehat{V}_A\geq V_E.
\]

Expected directional properties will also be tested numerically:

- put value decreases as spot increases;
- put value increases as strike increases;
- put value generally increases as volatility increases;
- American value does not fall below the corresponding European value.

These properties will be measured, not assumed.

---

# 10. Experimental design and leakage controls

The data will be divided into:

1. training set;
2. validation set;
3. in-domain held-out test set;
4. out-of-domain test sets constructed from entire parameter regions excluded from training.

Possible out-of-domain regimes include very high volatility, deep in-the-money or out-of-the-money puts, long maturities, high rates, and unusual rate–dividend combinations.

All normalization parameters will be estimated exclusively from the training data and applied unchanged to validation and test data.

# 12. Anticipated limitations

- Synthetic labels inherit the assumptions and approximation errors of the generating model.
- Constant volatility does not reproduce market volatility smiles or term structures.
- Continuous dividends do not capture discrete corporate dividend events.
- The benchmark is a numerical approximation rather than an observable true value.
- Random sampling may underrepresent economically important boundary regions.
- A model may interpolate accurately but extrapolate poorly.
- Inference-speed comparisons exclude the up-front cost of training and data generation.
- Low average error may hide failures close to the early-exercise boundary.
